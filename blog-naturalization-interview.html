What Happens at the U.S. Naturalization Interview?

A step-by-step overview of the interview, the questions an officer may review, the English and civics tests, and what can happen afterward.

By the OmniCitizen Editorial Team · Updated November 24, 2026 · 8–10 minute read

The naturalization interview is an important part of applying for U.S. citizenship. It is usually more than a civics quiz: a U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) officer also reviews your Form N-400, asks about your background, and checks information related to your application.

Knowing the general sequence can help you prepare calmly. Your appointment notice and instructions from USCIS should always take priority over a general online guide.

At a glance
  • Bring the documents listed in your interview notice and USCIS checklist.
  • Expect the officer to review your N-400 and ask about your answers.
  • Unless exempt, you will take the required English and civics tests.
  • Some cases need additional review or evidence.
  • You become a U.S. citizen only after taking the Oath of Allegiance.

1. Before the interview: read your appointment notice

USCIS sends an interview appointment notice with the date, time, and location. Read it carefully and follow any case-specific instructions. If you cannot attend, use official USCIS instructions to ask about rescheduling rather than simply missing the appointment.

Before interview day, review the complete copy of your Form N-400 and the documents you submitted. Be ready to explain your answers accurately. If something has changed since filing, do not guess about how to handle it; check USCIS instructions or seek qualified immigration advice when needed.

2. What documents should you bring?

USCIS says applicants should bring these items to the naturalization interview:

  • Your interview appointment notice.
  • Your Form I-551, Permanent Resident Card (Green Card).
  • A state-issued identification document, such as a driver’s license.
  • All valid and expired passports and travel documents issued to you that document your absences from the United States since becoming a permanent resident.

Other documents may be needed depending on your answers and circumstances. Review Form M-477, Document Checklist, and the instructions in your appointment notice.

3. The officer reviews your Form N-400

During the interview, the officer asks questions about your application and background. You may be asked to confirm details such as personal information, addresses, employment, travel outside the United States, marital history, and other answers on your N-400.

Listen carefully. Answer truthfully and directly. If you do not understand a question, politely ask the officer to repeat or clarify it. Do not memorize answers that are not true for your situation.

A useful preparation exercise is to read your submitted N-400 line by line and practise explaining each answer in your own words. Pay particular attention to dates, trips, and any answer that may have changed since filing.

4. The English test

Unless an exemption applies, the English portion evaluates reading, writing, and speaking. Speaking is assessed through your responses during the interview. The reading and writing portions are conducted as part of the test.

Practise understanding common questions and answering in clear, simple English. You do not need long speeches. Focus on understanding what is being asked and giving an accurate response.

Some applicants may qualify for an exemption or accommodation. The rules depend on the applicant’s circumstances and applicable USCIS requirements. Check the USCIS exceptions and accommodations guidance.

5. The civics test

The civics test covers U.S. history and government. The applicable test version and its rules depend on USCIS policy and the date and circumstances of your application. Before studying, verify which test applies to you using the USCIS test updates page.

Do not rely only on old practice materials or unofficial question lists. Use official USCIS study materials for the test version that applies to your case. Some answers can change when elected officials or officeholders change, so check current answers when preparing.

For a structured study routine, see How to Build a Civics Study Plan for Your U.S. Naturalization Test.

6. What happens after the interview?

After the interview and tests, USCIS will provide information about the result or next step. A decision may not always be made on the same day. USCIS may continue the case if additional evidence or another step is needed.

Read every notice carefully and respond by the deadline if USCIS asks for more information. Keep copies of anything you submit and use official USCIS channels to check your case status.

7. The Oath of Allegiance: the final step

If your application is approved, USCIS will schedule you for a naturalization ceremony. You are not a U.S. citizen simply because you passed the interview or received an approval. You become a citizen after taking the Oath of Allegiance at the naturalization ceremony.

At the ceremony, follow the instructions in your notice. USCIS will review required information, collect your Permanent Resident Card, administer the oath, and provide your Certificate of Naturalization after the ceremony process.

8. A practical interview-preparation checklist

  • Read your interview notice and confirm the location and time.
  • Review the exact N-400 application you submitted.
  • Prepare the identity, Green Card, passport, and travel documents requested by USCIS.
  • Check Form M-477 for additional documents relevant to your circumstances.
  • Practise answering N-400 questions truthfully in English, if the English requirement applies.
  • Study the correct civics test version using official USCIS materials.
  • Plan your trip to the USCIS office and allow time for arrival and check-in.
  • Keep notices and copies of submitted documents in one organised folder.
