Rights and Responsibilities of U.S. Citizens: What You Need to Know

Citizenship brings important freedoms, opportunities, and civic duties. Learn the difference between rights protected for people generally and privileges or responsibilities associated with U.S. citizenship.

By the OmniCitizen Editorial Team · Updated November 24, 2026 · 8–10 minute read

U.S. citizenship is more than a legal status. It connects people to a constitutional system and gives citizens opportunities to participate in public life. It also comes with responsibilities, such as respecting the law, serving on a jury when called, and supporting the Constitution.

One important distinction is that many constitutional freedoms belong to people living in the United States, not only citizens. Other rights—such as voting in federal elections—are reserved for U.S. citizens. The exact rules can depend on the right and the applicable federal, state, or local law.

Key distinction

Freedom of speech and freedom of religion are examples of rights generally protected for people in the United States. Voting in federal elections and running for federal office are examples of rights reserved for U.S. citizens, subject to constitutional and legal qualifications.

1. Rights and opportunities of U.S. citizens

USCIS identifies several important rights and opportunities associated with citizenship. These include:

  • Voting in federal elections: U.S. citizens can vote for federal elected officials when they meet applicable registration and voting requirements.
  • Running for federal office: Citizenship is required for federal elected offices, although the Constitution sets additional eligibility requirements for particular offices.
  • Applying for a U.S. passport: Citizens may apply for a U.S. passport and request appropriate U.S. government assistance while abroad.
  • Access to certain federal jobs: Some positions with the federal government require U.S. citizenship.
  • Petitioning for eligible family members: U.S. citizens may petition for certain relatives, and some family categories receive immigration priority compared with corresponding categories for permanent residents.
  • Security of the right to remain in the United States: A U.S. citizen generally has the right to live in the United States and cannot be deported from the country.

These are examples, not a complete list. Eligibility requirements still apply. For example, a passport application requires proof of citizenship and compliance with passport rules; it is not issued automatically.

2. Rights protected for people living in the United States

Some important freedoms are not exclusive to citizens. Depending on the constitutional provision and circumstances, people in the United States generally have protections that include:

  • Freedom of expression and speech.
  • Freedom to worship—or not to worship—according to personal belief.
  • Freedom of assembly and the right to petition the government.
  • Important legal protections, including due process and fair treatment under the law.

For civics-test preparation, pay attention to questions that ask for rights of everyone living in the United States. Do not confuse those with questions asking for a right that is only for U.S. citizens.

3. Civic responsibilities

Citizenship also involves responsibilities that help the constitutional system and communities function. USCIS educational materials highlight responsibilities such as:

  • Support and defend the Constitution: Understand the principles of the country’s constitutional system and respect its laws.
  • Obey the law: Follow applicable federal, state, and local laws.
  • Pay taxes honestly and on time: Meet tax obligations that apply to your circumstances.
  • Serve on a jury when called: Respond to a valid jury summons and follow the court’s instructions. Jury eligibility and exemptions are governed by applicable law.
  • Stay informed and participate: Learn about public issues, take part in the democratic process, and contribute to your community.
  • Respect other people: Respect the rights, beliefs, and opinions of others, including people with different backgrounds.
  • Defend the country if required: The Oath of Allegiance includes a promise to support and defend the Constitution and to perform certain service if required by law.

4. Voting: a right and an important civic activity

U.S. citizens can vote in federal elections if they satisfy the applicable requirements, including voter-registration rules. Voting gives citizens a way to choose representatives and influence public decisions.

Voting is widely described as an important civic responsibility, but federal law does not generally require every citizen to vote in every election. State and local election procedures differ, so voters should check official election information for their jurisdiction.

5. Jury service

Jury service is another important responsibility associated with citizenship. A jury listens to evidence and helps decide a case under the judge’s instructions. If you receive a jury summons, read it carefully and follow the court’s directions. Courts explain how to respond if you believe you qualify for an exemption or need to request a postponement.

6. Selective Service: understand the rule accurately

Selective Service registration is a separate legal requirement that is often mentioned in citizenship study materials. In general, most men who are U.S. citizens or immigrants living in the United States must register when they are 18 through 25 years old, subject to the rules and exceptions administered by the Selective Service System.

Registration is not the same as being drafted into military service. The United States currently has an all-volunteer military. A possible draft would require separate government action. For an individual determination, check the official Selective Service eligibility guidance.

7. The Oath of Allegiance

Applicants who complete naturalization generally take the Oath of Allegiance at a naturalization ceremony. The oath includes promises such as giving up prior allegiance to other countries, supporting and defending the Constitution and laws of the United States, and bearing arms or performing noncombatant service or work of national importance when required by law.

The oath is a formal commitment. The naturalization process is not complete—and the applicant does not become a U.S. citizen—until the oath is taken, unless a specific legal exception applies.

9. Practise the related civics questions

Use these prompts to check whether you understand the main ideas. The wording below is for learning and is not a substitute for the official USCIS question list.

  1. Name one right only for U.S. citizens.
  2. Name one responsibility that is only for U.S. citizens.
  3. Name two rights of everyone living in the United States.
  4. What is one promise you make when you become a U.S. citizen?
  5. What are two ways Americans can participate in their democracy?

For the applicable official test questions and accepted answers, use the USCIS study materials for your test version. Start with our civics study-plan guide and practise with the OmniCitizen learning simulator.
